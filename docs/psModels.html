6  Theories, models and techniques for problem solving

This chapter presents three classic models of problem solving — Dewey’s reflective thinking model, the Socratic model and Newell and Simon’s problem space model — followed by the main group techniques used in organisations (Six Thinking Hats and brainstorming) and creativity-based techniques, including lateral thinking and the management of cognitive biases.

6.1 Reflective thinking model

Dewey (1933) defined reflective thinking as ‘active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed form of knowledge in the light of the grounds that support it and the further conclusions to which it leads’. Reflection is not passive pondering but active inquiry: the thinker examines evidence for beliefs, considers alternative interpretations and follows reasoning to its conclusions. The cycle does not end with action — reflection on results feeds back into revised understanding (see also Schön, 1983).

6.1.1 Seven-step reflective thinking process

  1. Define the problem: clearly identify what is problematic. The initial ‘felt difficulty’ must be precisely articulated before effective analysis can begin.
  2. Analyse the problem: examine the available evidence. What is known? What is uncertain? What assumptions are being made?
  3. Establish criteria: define what a satisfactory solution would look like before generating options.
  4. Consider possible solutions: generate multiple candidates without premature evaluation. Intellectual openness at this stage is essential.
  5. Decide on a solution: evaluate candidates against the established criteria and select the solution best supported by evidence and reasoning.
  6. Implement the solution: execute the chosen course of action with awareness that implementation generates new information.
  7. Follow up: monitor outcomes. Results feed back into a revised understanding of the problem.

6.2 The Socratic model of problem solving

Socrates employed questioning (elenchus) to expose contradictions in learners’ thoughts and guide them toward deeper understanding. Rather than lecturing, he asked apparently naive questions that forced his interlocutors to examine and revise beliefs they had held unquestioned. This approach — dialectical thinking — engages productively with contradictions to synthesise new understanding.

6.2.1 What Socratic questioning cultivates

  • Clarification: ‘What exactly do you mean?’ ‘Can you give an example?’
  • Probing assumptions: ‘What are you assuming?’ ‘Is this assumption always valid?’
  • Probing evidence: ‘What evidence supports this?’ ‘Are there alternative interpretations?’
  • Exploring implications: ‘If this is true, what follows?’ ‘What are the consequences?’

6.2.2 The three-stage dialectical mechanism

  1. Identify contradictions: recognise where current assumptions clash with observed reality.
  2. Explore tensions: understand why the contradictions exist — what different values or information sources underlie each position?
  3. Synthesise: build a new, more comprehensive understanding that transcends the original contradiction.

6.3 Newell and Simon’s problem space model

Newell & Simon (1972) treat problem solving as a search process within a problem space — the complete set of possible states and transition pathways between the initial situation and the goal. The model laid the foundation for modern cognitive psychology and artificial intelligence. Its main concepts are illustrated in Table 6.1.

6.3.1 Key heuristic strategies

  • Means–end analysis: identify the largest gap between the current and goal state and select the operator most likely to reduce it; repeat until the goal is reached.
  • Hill climbing: always choose the operator that appears to move most directly toward the goal. This is efficient but prone to local optima.
  • Working backwards: begin from the goal state and reason backwards to identify the required prior steps.

6.3.2 Critiques

  • Well-structured bias: the model works best for problems with clearly defined states (puzzles, games) and is less effective for ill-structured problems, where goals and operators are ambiguous.
  • Neglect of emotion and culture: the model focuses purely on cognition; collective, culturally situated problem solving requires different frameworks.

6.4 Problem-solving techniques in organisations

6.4.1 Six Thinking Hats

De Bono’s (1985) Six Thinking Hats method addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional group discussion: people mix information, emotion, logic and creativity in ways that generate conflict rather than insight. The method separates different types of thinking in time — everyone thinks in the same direction simultaneously, but the direction changes as hats are ‘switched’. This parallel-thinking approach reduces defensiveness and generates richer analysis (Table 6.2).

Recommended sequence for critical thinking
  1. White Hat — establish the factual baseline;
  2. Yellow Hat — identify genuine benefits;
  3. Black Hat — identify genuine risks;
  4. Green Hat — generate creative alternatives that capture the benefits while avoiding the risks;
  5. Red Hat — capture gut reactions to the alternatives;
  6. Blue Hat — summarise and plan the next steps.

Each round is time-limited to 2–5 minutes to maintain energy and focus.

6.4.2 Brainstorming

Osborn (1953) observed that conventional group meetings suppressed creativity because participants feared criticism and self-censored unconventional ideas. His solution was to separate idea generation from evaluation — suspending judgement to create psychological safety for unconventional thinking. Later research on group creativity has examined the conditions under which such groups actually outperform individuals (see Paulus & Nijstad, 2003). The use of brainstorming in decision making is discussed in Section 4.3.

Five core principles

  1. Focus: maintain a clear focus on the specific problem. A brainstorm that drifts loses its generative power.
  2. Suspended judgement: no criticism during generation — not even through body language. All ideas are recorded.
  3. Personal safety: participants must be confident that expressing a ‘silly’ idea will not damage their standing.
  4. Serial discussion: one person speaks at a time, which ensures that all ideas are heard and that quieter participants contribute.
  5. Build on ideas: combine, modify and extend others’ contributions. The most valuable ideas in a brainstorm are often hybrids.

Three approaches to brainstorming

  • Visioning: imagine an ideal future unconstrained by current resources. Techniques include the Wish List, the Ideal Scenario and the Time Machine (‘It is 2040 and this problem has been brilliantly solved — what did we do?’).
  • Modifying: systematically examine current practices and ask how each element could be improved within real constraints. Customer utility maps identify pain points at each stage of the user experience.
  • Experimenting: combine existing elements in novel ways — ‘What if we combined SMS advisory with community listening groups?’ This is innovation through recombination rather than radical invention.

6.5 Creativity-based problem-solving techniques

6.5.1 Lateral thinking

Lateral thinking describes a deliberate approach to generating ideas by attacking problems from unexpected angles rather than following the conventional logic of ‘vertical thinking’ (de Bono, 1970). Vertical thinking deepens an existing line of reasoning; lateral thinking abandons it in search of a different starting point (Table 6.3).

6.5.2 Lateral thinking techniques

  • Random word: introduce an unrelated, randomly selected word and force connections to the problem. This short-circuits habitual thinking patterns.
  • Provocative operation (Po): make a deliberately outrageous statement — ‘Po: Farmers should pay for extension services with crops, not money.’ The provocation can lead to practical ideas such as value-sharing arrangements.
  • Reversal: reverse the problem. ‘How do we stop farmers from wanting to attend meetings?’ often reveals assumptions that are invisible in the original framing.
  • Challenge: systematically question every assumption in the current approach. Why does extension advice have to be delivered by trained agents? Why must training be in person?

6.5.4 Cognitive biases in problem solving

Judgement under uncertainty is shaped by systematic biases (Kahneman, 2011; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Four are especially relevant to agricultural problem solving:

  • Gambler’s fallacy: the mistaken belief that past random events influence future random events — for example, believing that a drought is ‘due’ because it has not rained for weeks. Agricultural risk management requires an understanding of statistical independence.
  • Winner’s curse: the tendency to over-invest in a solution because competitive dynamics create pressure, regardless of whether it is appropriate for local conditions.
  • Confirmation bias: attending to evidence that confirms pre-existing beliefs while discounting contrary evidence. An agent convinced that a particular variety is superior will unconsciously filter new information accordingly.
  • Availability heuristic: judging the probability of an event by how easily examples come to mind. A dramatic crop failure will seem more likely to recur than the statistics justify.
