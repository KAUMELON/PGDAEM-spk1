5  Problem solving in agriculture and extension organisations

Problem solving is central to human activity in every field, but it takes on particular significance in agriculture and extension work, where poor decisions can affect livelihoods, food security and environmental sustainability. This chapter defines a problem, examines the problems extension workers face, sets out process frameworks for agricultural problem solving, and distinguishes problem solving from decision making.

5.1 What is a problem?

Linhart (1976) defines a problem as an interactive relation between a subject and its surroundings, incorporating an inner conflict that is resolved by searching for transitions from an initial condition to a final, desired condition (the goal). A problem is therefore not merely a technical obstacle — it is a relational phenomenon arising in the gap between a person’s current capacity and the demands of their situation.

5.1.1 Three core properties of a problem

Linhart (1976) identifies three core properties of a problem:

  • Inner conflict as driver: the tension between current and desired states is not merely intellectual — it is motivational. In agricultural extension, unresolved problems such as crop losses or declining incomes are the motivational drivers for seeking new approaches.
  • Conflict creates activity: without the tension of an unsolved problem there is no impetus to act. The problem’s inner conflict is the engine of purposeful behaviour.
  • Transcending the given: the problem solver must exceed what is directly stated, searching beyond the current situation and the available information. This distinguishes genuine problem solving from routine task execution.

5.1.2 The problematic situation

Linhart (1976) defines the problematic situation as the totality of conditions that determine the formation and specific characteristics of a problem. These conditions may be static or dynamic, and their nature fundamentally shapes what kinds of solutions are possible (Table 5.1).

Key insight

In agriculture, most problems are dynamic rather than static. Rainfall, pest pressure, soil fertility, market prices and policy environments change simultaneously. Effective agricultural problem solving must be adaptive, iterative and grounded in ongoing observation.

5.1.3 Features of dynamic problem solving

The difficulties of acting in complex, changing situations have been widely documented (see Dörner, 1997). In agriculture, dynamic problem solving has the following features:

  • Change over time: problem conditions evolve continuously — a minor pest outbreak can escalate rapidly, and a drought can become a food security crisis within a season.
  • External influences: agricultural problems are shaped by natural forces, market dynamics, policy environments and technological change — none of which extension workers control.
  • Limited control: practitioners can often only respond to changes rather than prevent them, making adaptive capacity as important as pre-planned solutions.
  • Ongoing monitoring: continuous observation and adjustment are required; static plans rarely survive contact with actual conditions unchanged.
  • Positive potential: dynamic change occasionally reveals unexpected opportunities — late rains saving a wilting crop, or price spikes making a previously unviable crop profitable.

5.2 Problem solving in extension organisations

5.2.1 The challenge for extension workers

Extension workers serve as the bridge between scientific knowledge systems and farming communities, operating simultaneously in institutional, social, technical and political domains (see Swanson & Rajalahti, 2010). As environments change rapidly — driven by technology, climate change, market liberalisation and shifting government priorities — the problems extension workers face become more complex and less predictable. At its core, a problem exists when an individual has a goal but lacks a clear method to achieve it (Newell & Simon, 1972). This distinguishes genuine problem solving from the routine application of learned procedures.

5.2.2 The five-step problem-solving process

A widely used sequential framework sets out five steps (Bormann & Bormann, 1988):

  1. Define the problem clearly: describe the gap between the current and desired situation in specific, observable terms without implying solutions.
  2. Formulate goals and criteria: establish what a good solution looks like before generating options. Criteria anchor the subsequent evaluation process.
  3. Generate hypothetical solutions: produce multiple candidate solutions without premature evaluation. Quantity and variety at this stage improve the eventual quality of the choice.
  4. Collect and analyse data: gather evidence that distinguishes between candidate solutions and confirms or disconfirms hypothesised causes.
  5. Test solutions against criteria: evaluate each candidate, select the best fit, implement and monitor.

5.2.3 Problems of county extension agents: the West Virginia survey

A statewide survey of 48 extension agents across 38 counties of West Virginia in 1965 identified 126 problems (Table 5.2). The two most frequently cited — motivating leaders (18%) and motivating the public (16%) — are both people-problems requiring psychological and sociological insight, not technical knowledge alone.

5.2.4 The importance of problem definition

How a problem is framed determines what solutions appear possible. Albert Einstein is often quoted as observing that the formulation of a problem is more essential than its solution. Extension workers must therefore invest time in definition before rushing to solutions.

The power of reframing

Original framing: ‘How can we reach young homemakers?’ — this focuses on the extension worker’s logistics.

Reframed: ‘How can we free up time for young homemakers to attend meetings?’ — this reveals the real barrier: childcare.

Implication: providing childcare during meetings immediately becomes the solution.

Rule: frame problems in terms of controllable elements. ‘Not enough time’ is not actionable; task prioritisation and delegation are.

5.2.5 People-problems and the danger of mental set

Working with human attitudes and motivations is fundamentally different from diagnosing a crop disease. Extension workers need psychological and sociological concepts as analytical tools, not just agricultural expertise (Van den Ban & Hawkins, 1996).

Mental set — the tendency to apply familiar thinking patterns even when they no longer fit — is a major obstacle. Classic riddles illustrate this: ‘A farmer has 20 cows. All but 13 die. How many remain?’ (Answer: 13, not 7.) Extension workers must guard against applying solutions that worked in one context to structurally different problems. Strategies for overcoming mental set include:

  • Asking clarifying questions: ‘What exactly is the problem?’ ‘What are we assuming that we have not verified?’
  • Reasoning by analogy: how have other fields — health promotion, community organising — solved comparable problems of reaching disengaged populations?
  • Seeking diverse perspectives: people from different backgrounds hold different mental sets; cross-functional problem definition almost always reveals dimensions any one group would miss (Rogers, 2003).

5.3 Problem solving: process and framework in agriculture

5.3.1 A systems thinking approach

Agricultural problems are embedded in living systems characterised by complexity, variability and uncertainty. Effective agricultural problem solving must therefore be systematic while remaining flexible enough to accommodate inherent unpredictability. Systems thinking — understanding how components interact and how system-level properties emerge from these interactions — provides an essential conceptual foundation (Checkland, 1981). Its key principles are:

  • Feedback loops: actions generate consequences that loop back to influence future conditions. Excess fertiliser increases yield in the short term but degrades soil in the long term — a delayed negative feedback that a narrow analysis would miss.
  • Emergence: system-level problems (e.g., declining watershed health) emerge from many individual actions, none of which individually causes the outcome.
  • Delays: the consequences of agricultural decisions often appear long after the decision, making causal connections difficult to identify without systematic tracking.
  • Leverage points: some interventions produce disproportionately large improvements. Identifying high-leverage points is a core skill in agricultural problem solving (Senge, 1990).

5.4 Problem solving and decision making: key differences

Problem solving and decision making are related but distinct processes. Decision making is embedded within the larger process of problem solving — specifically during the ‘evaluate and choose’ stage. Problem solving asks ‘what is wrong and how can it be fixed?’; decision making asks ‘which of these available options should I choose?’ Decision making without prior problem solving risks selecting solutions to the wrong problem; problem solving without rigorous decision making risks an inability to commit to a course of action (see March, 1994). 5.4.1 Programmed and non-programmed decisions

Simon (1960) distinguishes two kinds of decisions:

  • Programmed decisions: routine, repetitive decisions for which standard procedures already exist — for example, an extension agent following an established pesticide application recommendation protocol.
  • Non-programmed decisions: novel, complex decisions requiring original analysis and judgement — for example, deciding how to respond to a newly emerged plant disease with no established recommendation.

Effective extension managers invest appropriate analytical effort and resist applying programmed responses to non-programmed situations — a temptation strengthened by the human preference for fast, intuitive judgement (Kahneman, 2011).
