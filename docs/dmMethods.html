4  Methodologies for decision making in extension organisations

This chapter describes the main methodologies used to structure decisions in agriculture and extension organisations: decision analysis and decision trees, the Delphi technique, brainstorming, the cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram, data-driven decision making and decision support systems.

4.1 Decision analysis and decision trees

4.1.1 Decision analysis

Decision analysis is the systematic evaluation of alternatives using quantitative and qualitative tools. It involves defining objectives, identifying alternatives, assessing outcomes and selecting the best option (Hardaker et al., 2015). Decision analysis is applied both at farm level — deciding which crop to grow — and at organisational level — determining which extension programme to fund.

4.1.2 The decision tree

A decision tree is a visual, tree-shaped diagram that maps decisions, chance events and their possible outcomes. Its key components are:

  • Decision nodes (squares) — where the decision maker chooses among alternatives;
  • Chance nodes (circles) — where outcomes depend on probability;
  • Branches — representing decisions or chance outcomes; and
  • Terminal nodes — end points showing the final payoff.

4.1.3 Method

  1. Define the decision problem clearly.
  2. Identify all possible decision alternatives.
  3. Identify chance events and assign probabilities.
  4. Assign monetary values or utility scores to each terminal node.
  5. Calculate the Expected Monetary Value (EMV): multiply each probability by its outcome value and sum the products.
  6. Select the decision branch with the highest EMV.
Agricultural example: to irrigate or not

A farmer decides whether to irrigate paddy or rely on rainfall (Figure 4.1).

  • Irrigation: cost Rs. 5,000; guaranteed yield of 4 t/ha regardless of rain.
  • Rainfall: 60% chance of adequate rain (yield 3.5 t/ha; revenue Rs. 35,000) and 40% chance of drought (yield 1.5 t/ha; revenue Rs. 15,000).

EMV of relying on rainfall = (0.6 × 35,000) + (0.4 × 15,000) = Rs. 21,000 + Rs. 6,000 = Rs. 27,000.

At the same price (Rs. 10,000 per tonne), irrigation gives a certain revenue of Rs. 40,000, or Rs. 35,000 net of its cost. The decision tree allows the farmer to compare the two branches directly; in this example irrigation is the better choice.

4.2 The Delphi technique

The Delphi technique is a structured expert consultation method used to achieve consensus on complex issues through multiple rounds of anonymous questionnaires (Linstone & Turoff, 1975). It is particularly useful when there is no single correct answer and when expert judgement is required to address a question on which objective data are scarce.

4.2.1 Method

  1. Define the problem or question clearly.
  2. Select a panel of relevant experts (farmers, scientists, extension specialists, policymakers).
  3. Distribute the Round 1 questionnaire anonymously.
  4. Analyse responses and identify areas of agreement and disagreement.
  5. Prepare a feedback summary and distribute the Round 2 questionnaire.
  6. Repeat rounds until consensus emerges or positions stabilise.
  7. Compile a final report reflecting consensus or documented divergence.

4.3 Brainstorming in decision making

Brainstorming — covered in detail as a problem-solving technique in Section 6.4.2 — is also used as a decision-making technique when generating decision alternatives, identifying barriers to adoption and developing extension programme ideas. Its core rules are to defer judgement, encourage wild ideas, build on others’ ideas and go for quantity (Osborn, 1953). In extension it is used for identifying new programme approaches, solving field problems encountered by agents, staff planning and annual review sessions, and identifying barriers to technology adoption.

4.4 Cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram in decision making

The fishbone diagram — also covered as a problem-solving tool in Section 7.7 — serves a complementary role in decision making by structuring the diagnosis of why a decision problem exists (Ishikawa, 1990). Using the 6M framework (Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Mother Nature), it organises potential causes systematically before decision alternatives are generated. In extension it is used for diagnosing why a technology adoption programme underperformed, investigating causes of low crop yield, analysing reasons for poor attendance at training programmes, and improving the quality of service delivery.

4.5 Data-driven decision making (DDDM)

Data-driven decision making (DDDM) refers to basing decisions on the analysis of data rather than on intuition alone (Jones et al., 2017). In agricultural extension, DDDM enables evidence-based programme design, resource allocation and performance monitoring. The main data sources in agricultural extension are:

  • farm surveys and household data;
  • remote sensing and satellite imagery;
  • weather and climate data from meteorological agencies;
  • market price data and value chain analysis; and
  • extension programme monitoring and evaluation reports.

Decision support built on these sources may be data-driven or document-driven (Table 4.2).

Quick revision

  • Decision analysis → objectives, alternatives, outcomes, best option.
  • Decision tree → squares (decisions), circles (chance), branches, terminal payoffs; choose the highest EMV.
  • EMV → Σ (probability × outcome value).
  • Delphi (Linstone and Turoff) → anonymous expert rounds with feedback until consensus.
  • Brainstorming (Osborn) → defer judgement, wild ideas, build on others, quantity.
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) → 6Ms; diagnose why before choosing.
  • DDDM → decisions from data; DSS → databases + models + interfaces (DSSAT, AquaCrop, SARRA-H, iSAT).
