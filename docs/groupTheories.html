3  Group decision making and theories of decision making

This chapter examines why and how groups make decisions in extension organisations, the strategies available to extension managers, and the main theories that explain how farmers and organisations actually decide.

3.1 Why group decision making matters in extension

Many of the most consequential decisions in agricultural extension — programme prioritisation, community intervention design, technology assessment — involve multiple stakeholders with diverse interests, knowledge bases and risk tolerances. Group decision-making processes, when well structured, harness this diversity to produce more robust decisions and generate the shared ownership essential for implementation (Leeuwis & Van den Ban, 2004).

3.4 Groupthink and its prevention

Groupthink occurs when the desire for harmony or conformity in a group overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives (Janis, 1982). Its symptoms include an illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalisation, pressure on dissenters and self-censorship. Prevention strategies include explicitly assigning a ‘devil’s advocate’ role, inviting external perspectives, allowing anonymous idea submission, and requiring each member to critically evaluate proposals before the final meeting.

3.5 Theories of decision making

3.5.1 Rational Choice Theory

Rational Choice Theory (RCT) holds that an actor identifies alternative actions, determines their consequences and chooses the option that optimally satisfies their preferences — specifically, the option with the highest expected utility (Arrow, 1951). Its meta-theoretical foundation is methodological individualism: social facts result from the interaction and aggregation of individual rational actors (Boudon & Betton, 1999). In farming, the primary objective is typically taken to be income maximisation (Edwards-Jones, 2006).

Case study: Organic farming as rational choice

Best (2009) conducted a postal survey of 657 farmers in western Germany to test a rational choice model of organic farming adoption using Subjective Expected Utility (SEU) theory.

Result: Subjectively expected utility explained the adoption decision well. Non-tangible consequences (daily working conditions, identity) were as relevant as financial returns.

Implication: Extension advisers must present technologies in terms of the full range of subjective values that farmers hold, not only economic returns.

3.5.2 Bounded rationality

H. A. Simon (Nobel Prize, 1978) introduced bounded rationality as a departure from RCT. Decision makers are constrained by limited information, limited cognitive processing capacity and limited time. As a result, they tend to satisfice — choosing options that are ‘good enough’ relative to an aspiration threshold — rather than optimise (Simon, 1955). Studies consistently show that bounded rationality provides a more realistic description of actual farmer decision making than perfect rationality (Wens et al., 2020).

Satisficing

The satisficing heuristic combines ‘satisfy’ and ‘suffice’. Rather than pursuing the absolute best option, the decision maker searches sequentially through alternatives and stops when one meets the aspiration threshold. For example, a household evaluating livelihood options stops searching when the first option guarantees at least 1,830 calories per person per day — the minimum food security threshold.

Fast and frugal decision-tree heuristics

Bounded rationality is also expressed through fast and frugal decision trees — simplified, rule-based methods that evaluate specific criteria in a predetermined sequence (Gigerenzer & Selten, 2001). Decision makers consider only selected pieces of information, opting for efficient shortcuts rather than exhaustive analysis. These heuristics are ecologically rational — they perform well because they are adapted to the statistical structure of their natural decision environment.

3.5.3 Theory of Planned Behaviour

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) proposes that behaviour is determined by behavioural intentions, which are in turn shaped by three components (Ajzen, 1991) (Figure 3.1):

  • Attitude: a positive or negative evaluation of performing the behaviour;
  • Subjective norms: perceived social pressure from important others; and
  • Perceived behavioural control: perception of the ease or difficulty of performing the behaviour.

TPB has been widely applied in agricultural extension research to predict farmers’ adoption intentions for new technologies and conservation practices.

3.5.4 Diffusion of Innovations theory

Rogers (2003) conceptualises technology adoption as a decision-making process unfolding across five stages: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation and confirmation. Farmers are classified as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority or laggards based on adoption timing. Extension agents must understand the characteristics and information needs of each adopter category to design appropriate communication and advisory strategies.

Quick revision

  • Group decisions → more information and buy-in; slower; better for complex, multi-stakeholder issues.
  • Strategies → authoritative, consultative, collaborative/participatory, delegative.
  • Groupthink (Janis) → conformity overrides appraisal; prevent with devil’s advocate, outsiders, anonymity.
  • Rational choice → highest expected utility; methodological individualism.
  • Bounded rationality (Simon) → satisficing; fast and frugal heuristics (Gigerenzer).
  • TPB (Ajzen) → attitude + subjective norms + perceived behavioural control → intention → behaviour.
  • Diffusion (Rogers) → knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation; five adopter categories.
